Can we call the Mosaic Covenant a republication of the Covenant of Works, or is there a better term? I believe we can, and I believe the [1689 Federalist](/1689-federalism) or Particular Baptist position gives us the clearest account of why, not as a theological novelty, but as the most careful reading of the covenantal structure of Scripture. Furthermore, I wanted to develop in this blog that the Mosaic Covenant is a covenant of works precisely for the gospel. It serves the purposes of the gospel, which is different from the original Covenant of Works with Adam. But first, let me lay out the purpose and function of this earthly, temporal, and national edition of the Covenant of Works. 

## The Purpose and Function of Republication

The confusion surrounding republication often comes from a failure to distinguish what the Mosaic Covenant was *for* from what it *looked like*. I think four features of the 1689 Federalist account, taken together, dissolve most of that confusion.

First, the republication was given as a "ministry of condemnation," not as a renewed pathway to eternal life. Man is "utterly incapable" of fulfilling a covenant of works as a means of justification, and the Mosaic economy did not pretend otherwise; it was given to manifest guilt and stop every mouth before God.[^1] Second, while man lost his power to obey through the Fall, God did not lose His right to command. The republication reminds sinners that they remain legally bound to a standard of perfect righteousness they cannot meet, and that this obligation does not dissolve simply because they lack the power to discharge it.[^2] Third, the 1689 Federalists carefully distinguish between the *end* of the original Covenant of Works (eternal life) and the *end* of its Mosaic edition, which was to govern a national theocracy in Canaan and to provide typical, earthly blessings contingent on external obedience.[^3] The confusion of these two ends, as though Sinai were simply Adam's covenant rereleased without qualification, is precisely the error 1689 Federalism is at pains to avoid. Fourth, and most remarkable to me: for Christ to merit righteousness for the elect, He had to be born "under the law," that is, under a legal covenant of works that rewarded perfect obedience. The Mosaic republication provided the historical and legal framework for Christ to fulfill the original Adamic terms on behalf of His people.[^4]

## The Nature and Purpose of the Law at Sinai

Scripture itself gives three distinct reasons for the giving of the law at Sinai. Each one answers a different question about why God placed Israel under this covenant structure, and each one points beyond the covenant to Christ.

### 1. To Expose and Condemn Sin (Gal. 3:19, 22; 2 Cor. 3:7–9; Rom. 3:19–20)

The law was not given as a renewed offer of life but as a prosecutor. Paul's argument in Galatians is explicit: the law "was added because of transgressions" (Gal. 3:19), not to provide a way through them, but to make them visible and to stop every mouth before God. "The Scripture imprisoned everything under sin" (Gal. 3:22); the law does not unlock the prison, it reveals that the prisoner is already there. So Paul again in Romans: "Now we know that whatever the law says it speaks to those who are under the law, so that every mouth may be stopped and the whole world may be held accountable to God. For by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight, since through the law comes knowledge of sin" (Rom. 3:19–20).

The same function is what gives Paul's contrast in 2 Corinthians its force: the old covenant is a "ministry of condemnation" and a "ministry of death" (2 Cor. 3:7–9), not because it was wicked, but because that is precisely what a covenant of perfect demand, imposed on a people utterly incapable of meeting it, must produce. It was given to manifest guilt, not to relieve it.

### 2. A Schoolmaster to Drive Sinners to Christ (Gal. 3:24; Heb. 10:1)

The law's condemning work was not its final word. Paul calls the law a *paidagogos*, a guardian or schoolmaster, "until Christ came, in order that we might be justified by faith" (Gal. 3:24). The condemned sinner, finding no relief in the law's demands, is driven to look elsewhere. The law evacuates the sinner of all self-righteousness and points, by its very severity, to the one who could fulfill what no Israelite could.

The writer of Hebrews makes the same point from the sacrificial system: "the law has but a shadow of the good things to come instead of the true form of these realities" (Heb. 10:1). The repeated offerings could never finally atone. By design. They trained Israel to see the inadequacy of every human approach to God and to hold out for the substance the shadows promised.

### 3. Legal Provision for Christ Born Under the Law (Gal. 4:4; Rom. 8:3–4)

There is a third function, peculiar to the 1689 Federalist reading, and I think it is the most theologically significant of the three. The Sinai covenant provided the legal economy necessary for Christ to fulfill the law on behalf of the elect. "When the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law" (Gal. 4:4). Christ was not merely born into a cultural context. He was born into a specific covenantal obligation. He could only fulfill and exhaust the law's demands by standing formally under them.

Paul draws the conclusion in Romans 8: "God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do. By sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh, in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us" (Rom. 8:3–4). The law's righteous requirement was not abolished. It was met, in Christ, for those who are in him.

These three scriptural purposes: condemnation, pedagogy, and legal provision for the second Adam, are what the Particular Baptists had in view when they called the Mosaic Covenant a republication of the Covenant of Works.[^5] These make the Mosaic not an obstacle to the gospel but its servant. The following quotations develop each of these in turn.

> "True, there was another Edition or Administration of it [the covenant of works] given to Israel, which tho' it was a covenant of works, i.e. *Do this and live*, yet it was not given by the Lord to the same End and Design, as the Covenant was given to our first parents, viz. It was not given to justifie them, or to give them eternal Life."
>
> — Benjamin Keach, *The Everlasting Covenant*[^6]

> "Indeed, I think, one great end of God in bringing Israel under this Sinai covenant, was to make way for Christ, his being born or made under the law, in order to the fulfilling of it for us. I do not see how (by any visible dispensation) Jesus Christ could have been born actually under the law, if this Sinai covenant had not been made."
>
> — Samuel Petto, quoted in Pascal Denault, *The Distinctiveness of Baptist Covenant Theology*[^7]

> "In the Mosaical economy, there was such a remembrance of the Covenant of Works revived, with the terms and sanction thereof; as that hereupon it is called the ministration of condemnation, and did engender unto bondage... But yet the promise of salvation by the Messiah... was not enervated thereby; but even this was laid in a subserviency to Gospel ends."
>
> — Nehemiah Coxe, *Vindiciae Veritatis*[^8]

> "Now this is no other but the covenant of works revived. Nor had this covenant of Sinai any promise of eternal life annexed to it, as such, but only the promise inseparable from the covenant of works which it revived, saying, 'Do this, and live.'"
>
> — John Owen, *An Exposition of the Epistle to the Hebrews*[^9]

> "The Mosaic Covenant is a covenant of obedience for life in the land of Canaan... The Mosaic Covenant echoes Adam's covenant in Eden and revives the Covenant of Works in key ways, but it is not the same covenant. The place is different, the parties are different, the promises are different, and the positive laws are entirely different."
>
> — Samuel D. Renihan, *The Mystery of Christ, His Covenant, and His Kingdom*[^10]

> "An open manifestation of the covenant of works was needed, not to kill, but to show all the world — Israel in particular — that they were already dead, sick, and in need of a physician... It did not bring about man's condemnation, but the manifestation of man's condemnation."
>
> — Jeffrey D. Johnson, *The Fatal Flaw of the Theology of the Federal Vision*[^11]

## A More Precise Term: The Subservient Covenant

My claim is that the Mosaic Covenant is a covenant of works for the gospel. However, the term "covenant of works" for the Mosaic Covenant, while not inaccurate, has consistently generated confusion among non-federalists. Earl Blackburn states the objection plainly:

> "Republicationism does not take into full consideration the true nature of the Covenant of Works… It confuses the nature of salvation and justification. Are salvation and justification by works, or by works and grace?"[^12]

I share this sentiment. The confusion is understandable. Especially when I talk to brothers who are sincere and want to understand the position. However, I want to point out that, often, when inquirers hear "covenant of works," they assume the claim is that fallen Israelites were expected to earn eternal life by perfect obedience, which would indeed be a confusion of law and gospel. But this is not the claim. The 1689 Federalist use of "covenant of works" for the Mosaic refers only to its *structure*, its *do this and live* sanctions governing national life in Canaan, not to its ultimate design. The works-character describes the *form*; it does not describe the *end*.

This is why the term *subservient covenant* (*foedus subserviens*), used by John Cameron, John Owen, Nehemiah Coxe, and Samuel Renihan, is more precise. It tells us not only what the Mosaic *looked like* but what it was *for*.

Philip Cary, who served as a representative for the Dartmouth congregation at the London Particular Baptist General Assemblies in 1689 and 1692,  pressed this point:

> "A subserviency in any thing to promote the ends of something else, does not make it to be the thing itself; the ends whereof are promoted thereby."[^19]

Subservience is a relation, not an identity. The Mosaic promoted the ends of the Covenant of Grace without becoming the Covenant of Grace. That is exactly the precision the debate requires. Just because it promotes the ends of the Covenant of Grace doesn't make it an administration of it.

John Owen states the distinction clearly:

> "Sinai should not then be thought of as the covenant of works; but Sinai does involve a renewal of the principles which partly constituted the covenant of works. On the other hand, the Sinai covenant cannot be thought of as the covenant of grace… It is a subservient covenant to the covenant of grace."[^13]

Coxe develops this at length. The national covenant made with Israel was neither the original Covenant of Works nor the Covenant of Grace; it was a third, distinct arrangement whose entire purpose was to serve the ends of the covenant of grace:

> "All the dealings of God with [Israel] as a select and peculiar People in Covenant with himself, were in Subserviency to the great Ends of this Covenant with Abraham… And that Covenant of Peculiarity is in the New Testament always styled Old, and Carnal; a Covenant that was to wax old and vanish away…"[^14]

> "The former was added and made subservient to the latter's great ends. The springs of new covenant mercy, which God had before opened to all nations, were not to be shut up again by this covenant… which was designed by God only to be a handmaid to it."[^15]

The language of "handmaid" is telling. The Mosaic covenant does not compete with the Covenant of Grace; it serves it. Its laws, its sanctions, its national blessings and curses, its sacrificial system: all of it was designed to hold the promise before Israel's eyes, discipline the nation toward the coming Seed, and drive the godly remnant to look beyond their external privileges to the substance those privileges shadowed. As Coxe puts it:

> "The State of Israel after the Flesh being typical; The Israel of God among them, were taught to look above, and beyond their external priviledges, unto those things that were shadowed by them, as set before their Faith in the promises of Grace by Christ; and so to live upon the Grace of that Covenant, which their outward State, and Covenant of Peculiarity was subservient to…"[^16]

Renihan captures the terminological history behind this precision. The term *foedus subserviens*, the subservient covenant, was coined to hold together what must be held together: that the Mosaic has a genuine works-structure (laws, promises, threats) while its ultimate design is grace-serving:

> "Based on the laws, the promises, and the threats of the covenant, the Mosaic Covenant was a covenant of works for life in the land of Canaan… Cameron stepped… to speak of the *vetus foedus sive foedus subserviens*, the old covenant or the subservient covenant."[^17]

Pascal Denault, summarizing the distinctive logic of 1689 Federalism on precisely this point, draws the contrast with the Westminster model in terms that make clear why "subservient" does the work that "covenant of works" alone cannot:

> "The 1689 federalism replaced the paedobaptist concept of one covenant of grace under different administrations by the one covenant of grace revealed by farther steps model. The understanding of this particular federalism is that the covenant of grace was not formally established during the O.T. period, but was revealed through the different covenants. Therefore, according to this view, the Old Covenant was both distinct from and subservient to the covenant of grace."[^18]

The phrase "both distinct from *and* subservient to" is the precise two-sided claim. "Covenant of works" captures only the first half: the Mosaic is distinct from, and structurally unlike, the Covenant of Grace. "Subservient covenant" captures both halves at once. Distinct from, yes, but also *serving*. That is the full 1689 Federalist position.

To call the Mosaic a "subservient covenant," then, is not to retreat from the republication thesis; it is to state it more carefully. The Mosaic *has* works-features: it republishes the law's demand, imposes sanctions, and operates on a do-this-and-live logic for national tenure in Canaan. But those works-features were not ends in themselves. They were the form of a covenant whose substance was always elsewhere, in the promises given to Abraham, fulfilled in Christ, and ratified in the New Covenant blood of the Mediator.

Thanks be to God because the Mosaic Covenant, though breakable and unable to give eternal life to those who belong to it, is not an end in itself. It points to something different and other than itself. It points to Christ, the Mediator of the New Covenant. 

Let the church recover this precision. The *foedus subserviens* is not a novelty of seventeenth-century Particular Baptist ingenuity; it is a retrieval of what the Scriptures themselves taught Israel to expect: a covenant that condemned and prepared, that served by its very severity the grace it could never itself deliver. How wonderful that the God who gave the law in thunder at Sinai is the same God who fulfilled it through the blood of the Lamb at Calvary.

Soli Deo Gloria!

## Further Reading

I mentioned John Cameron above as the coiner of *foedus subserviens*. For those who want to trace that history more rigorously, two resources are worth your time.

Richard Muller's article in the *Mid-America Journal of Theology* examines Cameron's covenant theology within its broader Reformed scholastic context, showing that the subservient covenant concept was not a novelty but a carefully argued position within the stream of post-Reformation orthodoxy. Muller's precision here is valuable: he resists both the dismissal of Cameron as an outlier and the anachronistic reading of him through later lenses. [Read the article (PDF).](https://www.midamerica.edu/files/pdf/journal/17-muller.pdf)

The Upper Register has a detailed study tracing the subservient covenant through Cameron, Samuel Bolton, and Moïse Amyraut, arguing that this 17th-century Reformed tradition anticipated much of what Meredith Kline later argued about the Mosaic economy. I find it a useful companion to the Particular Baptist sources, since it shows the view was not a Baptist invention but a retrieval from within the broader Reformed covenant tradition. [Read the study.](https://upper-register.com/topics/covenant-theology/subservient-cov/)
