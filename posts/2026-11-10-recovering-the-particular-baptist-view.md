It is undeniable that 1689 Federalism is a historical retrieval movement. Its central assertion — that the majority of the framers of the 1677/1689 Confession understood the New Covenant to *be* the Covenant of Grace, rather than merely one of its administrations — has been the subject of serious debate among Reformed Baptists. Scholars with PhDs in historical theology have done the retrieval work: they have gone to the sources, read the seventeenth-century Particular Baptists, and demonstrated that the framers of the Confession held a view of the covenants distinguishable from the Westminster tradition. That is a genuine and important contribution.

But retrieval is not enough. A movement that knows *what* the Particular Baptists confessed without knowing *why* they confessed it will inevitably reduce their theology to a set of propositions to defend rather than a way of reading Scripture to inhabit. The framers of the 1677/1689 Confession were not primarily confessionalists — they were biblical theologians. Their covenantal conclusions were driven by exegetical arguments, not the other way around. They moved from text to system, not from system back to text.

This essay argues that the genuine recovery of the Particular Baptist view requires recovering their biblical-theological method. We must ask not only what Nehemiah Coxe, Thomas Patient, or Benjamin Keach concluded about the New Covenant, but what drove them there — which texts, which exegetical moves, which theological inferences. Only then do we possess something worth passing on. We begin, as they did, with the New Covenant itself.

## I. The New Covenant Is the Covenant of Grace

The first and most foundational claim of Particular Baptist covenantal theology is not about the Mosaic Covenant or about circumcision and baptism. It is about the New Covenant: that the New Covenant *is* the Covenant of Grace — not an administration of it, not the final and fullest phase of a single covenant running through all redemptive history, but the Covenant of Grace itself, introduced at a specific moment in redemptive history, and enacted in the blood of Christ.

This claim carries most of its weight before you get to the debates. Establish it, and the covenantal structure of the Old Testament falls into place behind it. Fail to establish it, and no amount of appeal to the framers of the Confession will settle anything.

### Genesis 3:15 — The First Promise

The Particular Baptists did not begin the story of the Covenant of Grace at Sinai or even at Abraham. They began it at the fall. Thomas Patient, one of the most theologically precise of the early Particular Baptists, finds the substance of the New Covenant already present in the protevangelium:

> "This speech contains in substance, the Covenant of Grace. Christ the true spiritual seed being here promised, Who in Scripture, is held forth to be the very substance and marrow of the New Covenant."[^1]

What is crucial here is what Patient does not say. He does not say that Genesis 3:15 *inaugurates* the Covenant of Grace as a formal covenant. He says the promise contains, in substance, what the New Covenant is. The substance of the Covenant of Grace — Christ himself, promised as the seed — is present from the beginning. But a promise and a covenant are not the same thing. The formal covenant awaits its mediator, its ratification, its enactment. The promise runs through redemptive history. The covenant is enacted once, in the fullness of time, when Christ comes.

### Hebrews 8:6–13 / Jeremiah 31:31–34 — The Better Covenant, Enacted

If Genesis 3:15 gives the first promise, Hebrews 8 gives the clearest definition. The writer of Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31 at length to establish a single controlling argument: the New Covenant is *better* than the Mosaic — better in its mediator, better in its promises, and unlike the Old, it cannot be broken (Heb. 8:6–13). The Old is explicitly declared "obsolete" and "growing old" (v. 13); the New is established on the very promises God fulfills in his people, not promises his people make to him.

The Particular Baptists read this passage carefully — not as proof-texts for a confessional position, but as biblical data demanding covenantal distinctions.

**Verse 6 — "He is the Mediator of a better covenant, which has been enacted on better promises."**

Edward Hutchinson, writing in 1676 — one year before the 1677 edition of the Confession — pauses on the Greek verb the Spirit chose. The word translated "enacted" is νενομοθέτηται: legislated, ratified, brought formally into force. His observation is precise:

> "Before the new covenant, the covenant of grace was only *revealed*; when the new covenant was introduced, it was νενομοθέτηται [established/enacted, Heb. 8:6]… For the old house, or Jewish Church was not intended to abide for ever, but to the time of reformation, then the law must be changed, the priesthood changed, the privileges and ordinances changed, the seed changed, yea the Covenant changed."[^2]

This is a critical distinction that is hermeneutical before it is confessional. The Covenant of Grace was *revealed* through the old economy — promised, typified, and anticipated — but it was not *enacted* until the New Covenant was introduced. The old structures (law, priesthood, ordinances, seed) were never the Covenant of Grace itself; they were the scaffolding around the promise of it.

**Verses 7–8 — "For if that first covenant had been faultless, then no place would have been sought for a second."**

Benjamin Keach, writing in the year the 1689 Confession was adopted, draws the same line:

> "For if that first Covenant had been faultless, then should no place have been sought for the second; but finding fault with them, he saith, Behold the days come, saith the Lord, when I will make a new Covenant with the house of Israel, and the House of Judah, Heb. 8:7, 8… Therefore from hence I argue, that whatsoever external Rights or Privileges the Jews had under the old Covenant, it signifies just nothing to us, under the Dispensation of the Gospel…"[^3]

Keach's argument from verse 7 is a logical one native to the text: the need for a *new* covenant proves the inadequacy of the *old*. This is not anti-ceremonialism — it is what Hebrews explicitly states. And Keach draws the practical conclusion Hebrews intends: the external privileges of the old economy do not carry over. The covenant has changed, not merely its mode of administration.

**Verses 8–9 — "Not like the covenant that I made with their fathers… that covenant they broke."**

Patient sees in Hebrews 8:6–9 and Jeremiah 31:32 a proof that the Mosaic covenant was a *real* covenant — not a symbolic overlay of the Covenant of Grace, but its own distinct reality:

> "So in Heb. 8:6–9, 'But now hath he obtained a more excellent Ministry, by how much also he is the Mediator of a better Covenant, which is established upon better promises…' These Scriptures do evidence as clear as the sun at noon day, that there was a real covenant made with the Jews… This Covenant they broke, said Jeremiah 31:32."[^4]

The Mosaic covenant could be broken because it was conditioned on Israel's obedience. The New Covenant cannot be broken because God himself fulfills its conditions. That is not a difference of administration — it is a difference of nature.

**Verses 10–12 — Law written on the heart; "I will be their God"; sins remembered no more.**

The positive description of the New Covenant in Hebrews 8:10–12 — drawing directly from Jeremiah 31 — is where the Particular Baptists locate the full definition of the Covenant of Grace. Patient joins Hebrews 8 to Jeremiah 32:40 to show its everlasting character:

> "As in that eighth of Hebrews He engaged that He will write His Law in their hearts, and that He will be their God and they shall be His people… as Jer. 32:40, 'I will make an everlasting Covenant with them, that I will not turn away from them to do them good, but I will put my fear in their hearts that they shall not depart from me.'"[^5]

And Hercules Collins gives the full definition from this same passage:

> "Let us give a Definition of the Covenant of Grace, which is God's Promise of Justification, Sanctification and Glorification, unto all the Elect, upon the Account of Redemption and Satisfaction by Christ, in his Death and State of Humiliation; the Sum of this Covenant we have in Heb. 8. 10, 11, 12. God becomes a Peoples God for Christ's sake, hence his Blood is called the Blood of the New and Everlasting Covenant, because it was procured and ratified by it…"[^6]

What is striking is what Collins does not do: he does not start with a systematic definition and then verify it from Hebrews. He locates the *sum* of the Covenant of Grace in Hebrews 8:10–12 — in what the Spirit says through Jeremiah about the New Covenant. The definition flows from the text outward. The New Covenant *is* the Covenant of Grace because Hebrews says the New Covenant accomplishes exactly what the Covenant of Grace is: justification, sanctification, glorification — secured by Christ's blood, guaranteed by God alone.

### The Unconditional Character of the New Covenant

The identification of the New Covenant with the Covenant of Grace has a necessary corollary: the New Covenant is unconditional. Not in the sense that it demands nothing of its members — it demands everything. But in the sense that its fulfillment does not depend on the creature's faithfulness. Patient states this with characteristic precision:

> "It is impossible that the New Covenant can be broken because it is an absolute covenant made on no condition to be fulfilled by the creature, but God himself hath engaged to perform both parts of it, the commands and the promises, as the passages in Heb. 8 do evidence."[^7]

This is why the New Covenant cannot merely be a "better administration" of the same covenant structure as the Mosaic. The Mosaic covenant was broken — Patient established this from Jeremiah 31:32 above. The New Covenant cannot be broken. These are not two administrations of one covenant; they are covenants of different kinds.

### Promise and Covenant — A Necessary Distinction

One final terminological point before moving forward, because it is load-bearing for everything that follows. John Spilsbury, one of the earliest Particular Baptist theologians, insists on a distinction that the entire covenantal argument requires:

> "Every promise is not a covenant, there being a large difference between a promise and a covenant."[^8]

This matters because the objection to the Particular Baptist view often assumes that wherever there is a gospel promise in the Old Testament, the Covenant of Grace must formally be present. But Spilsbury's distinction cuts through this. A promise may anticipate a covenant; it is not therefore the covenant itself. The promise of the seed runs from Genesis 3:15 forward. The Covenant of Grace — formally, as a covenant — is enacted in Christ. The old economy was the age of promise and type. The New Covenant is the age of fulfillment and enactment. Hutchinson's νενομοθέτηται is the exegetical nail in that claim.
