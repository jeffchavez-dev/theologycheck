In Episode 269 of *Theology in Particular*, the podcast of International Reformed Baptist Seminary, Pastor Joe Anady interviews Dr. Daniel Scheiderer on the importance of Christian axioms.[^1] What follows is a summary of the key points and axioms discussed.

## What Is a Christian Axiom?

An axiom, in the technical sense, is a first principle — a starting point from which reasoning proceeds. Dr. Scheiderer uses the term more broadly to mean a pithy, condensed statement that captures an entire way of thinking about a particular Christian truth. It is not quite a definition, not quite a confession, but something that functions like both: a phrase so carefully formed that it can orient an entire argument, govern the way a doctrine is explained, and serve as a guardrail against error.

Christian axioms differ from what we might call "Christianese." Both are in-group language familiar to those inside the church. But Christianese is largely recent, culturally narrow, and theologically superficial — it does not govern the way we argue. Christian axioms, by contrast, are phrases that have crossed centuries, languages, cultures, and even denominational lines. They show up in the fourth century and the seventeenth. They appear in Calvin and in Keach. They surface in Reformed scholastics and in the Puritans. That breadth is what makes them axiomatic rather than merely colloquial.

The danger Scheiderer identifies is that many of these axioms have fallen into disuse over the last several generations. When we lose the phrases, we tend to lose the theological precision they carry. Recovering them is not an exercise in nostalgia — it is an exercise in doctrinal clarity.

## The Axioms

### Theology Proper

**"All that is in God is God."**
The full form of this axiom (truncated in the title of James Dolezal's *All That Is in God*) is a statement of divine simplicity. God is not a composition of parts. His goodness is not something distinct from God that he possesses — goodness, when attributed to God, names the one who simply *is*. This axiom governs how we speak about the divine attributes: we must never imply that there are things in God that are not God.

**"The works of God *ad extra* are undivided."**
*(Opera Dei ad extra sunt indivisa)*
Any work of God toward creation — in creation, in providence, in redemption — is a work of the undivided Trinity. It is not that the Father does one part, the Son another, and the Spirit a third. The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are the one undivided cause of every external effect. Scripture does appropriate particular works to particular persons (creation to the Father, redemption to the Son, sanctification to the Spirit), but appropriation is not exclusion. This axiom keeps Trinitarian language in Scripture from collapsing into a functional tritheism.

### Scripture

**"The faith once for all delivered to the saints."** *(Jude 3)*
The deposit of Christian truth is fixed. The Reformers drew on this axiom to insist that their work was *reform*, not innovation — they were restoring what had been deposited, not inventing something new.

**"Scripture alone is the sole and infallible authority"** — or in the scholastic idiom, **"the unnormed norming norm."**
The *norma normans non normata*: the rule that rules all other rules and is itself ruled by none. Scripture is the final court of appeal for all theological claims.

### Christology

**Theotokos — "Mother of God" / "Bearer of God."**
Mary bore God. The axiom was not coined to elevate Mary but to protect the unity of Christ's person against Nestorianism and adoptionism, which effectively drove a wedge between the man Jesus and the eternal Son. If the one in the womb of the Virgin is God, then she who bore him is, in that sense, the bearer of God. Elizabeth's words in Luke 1 — "the mother of my Lord" — give the axiom its biblical grounding.

**"God became man so that man might become God."**
This axiom from the church fathers states the purpose of the incarnation: not merely legal substitution (though that), but the exaltation of human nature toward the fullness of God-likeness. Scripture grounds it in 2 Peter 1:4 (partakers of the divine nature). We must be careful: this is not an Eastern Orthodox claim that we merge with the divine essence. It is the Reformed and patristic claim that glorification is a real elevation of human nature into the fullness of what it means to reflect God — what we shorten, every Sunday, into the word *godliness*.

**"That which is not assumed is not redeemed."**
If Christ did not assume a true human body, our bodies are not redeemed. If he did not assume a true human soul, our souls are not redeemed. The axiom cuts against Apollinarianism (which denied Christ a human rational soul) and Docetism (which denied a true human body). Its pastoral reach is broader than Christology: it grounds the bodily resurrection, refutes the gnostic notion of an immaterial eternity, and anchors the Lord's Supper as a physical means of grace.

**"He became what he was not without ceasing to be what he was."**
The eternal, infinite, unchanging Son took on finitude, weakness, and creaturely limitation — without ceasing to be eternal, infinite, and unchanging. The incarnation is an addition, not a subtraction or transformation. He did not shed divinity to become human; he assumed humanity while remaining fully divine.

### Soteriology

**"Sufficient for all, efficient for the elect."**
The atonement is not meager. Its limitation is not in power or worth — Christ's sacrifice is of infinite value and would be sufficient to cover the sins of every human being who has ever lived. Its limitation is in application: it is efficiently, actually applied to the elect alone. This axiom was used in medieval theology and carried forward by the Reformed, though its precise interpretation generated significant debate.

**"Justification is by faith alone."**
*Sola fide* — the Reformation's rallying cry. The axiom is not merely a slogan; it is a guardrail. It rules out every scheme of justification by works, merit, or infused grace. Rome's departure from it is, for the Reformers, the departure that reveals Rome's true theological colors.

### Ecclesiology

**"Outside the church, there is no salvation."**
*(Extra ecclesiam nulla salus)*
Salvation is not found in Islam, Hinduism, atheism, moralism, or false churches. Christ laid down his life for his sheep, his bride. The axiom comes from Cyprian in the third century — before the Roman ecclesiastical complex existed — and was never meant to identify salvation with communion to Rome. Its force is simpler and more fundamental: those whom God saves, he gathers into Christ's body. Scheiderer notes that the axiom does challenge the modern evangelical tendency to sharply separate the invisible and visible church, as if one can be invisibly united to Christ while having no visible connection to his people.

**"No one can have God as his father who does not have the church as his mother."**
Also from Cyprian, affirmed by Calvin, Turretin, Keach, and the Baptist Isaac Backus. Its biblical root is Galatians 4: the sons of promise have the heavenly Jerusalem — the church — as their mother, not the earthly Jerusalem of the circumcision party. The church begets spiritual children through the preaching of the gospel. She nurses them on the word. She raises them to maturity. Even the mature believer remains a child of this mother — still fed, still directed, still loved. Scheiderer notes Keach worked this image out at length in his *Typologia*.

## Why This Matters

Scheiderer's point throughout is not that we should drop these phrases in conversation to impress. The point is that these axioms, once internalized, actually *function* — they orient arguments, guard against error, and connect our present theology to the whole catholic tradition of the church. When you encounter one of them in an author you haven't read before, its presence is a signal: this author is working within a received tradition of carefully formed Christian speech. When you encounter a strange or unfamiliar axiom, the right response is not repulsion but curiosity: *What truth is this phrase trying to protect? What error was it coined to prevent?* The answer will almost always deepen your theology.

The recovery of Christian axioms is, in this sense, inseparable from the recovery of Christian catholicity.
