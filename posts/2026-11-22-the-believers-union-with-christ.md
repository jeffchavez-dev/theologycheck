The doctrine of union with Christ has been the foundational truth and reality for all of the believers' soteriological blessings. It is the glue that holds together the benefits of the Spirit's application of redemption towards sinners. As Petrus Van Mastricht writes, our "union with the Redeemer…is the proximate terminus to which faith strives" and "from union with Christ freely flows forth the communion of his benefits."[^1] Because believers are now united to Christ, Jesus' words that say, "and I give eternal life to them, and they will never perish; and no one will snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28), hold infallibly. It is in this reality of our union with Him that we can confidently say, together with the apostle Paul, that there is nothing in this world and in the world to come that "will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 8:39)

This paper attempts to articulate this glorious doctrine of the believer's union with Christ. By doing so, the following objectives are sought to be accomplished: First, a working definition of this doctrine will be provided, including its scriptural and historical-theological ramifications. Second, distinctions will be made among three aspects of the believer's union with Christ. Lastly, our mystical union with Christ, as emphasized in Scripture and by the older theologians, will be further examined, with a survey of its theological implications for other soteriological heads of doctrine, especially in relation to the *ordo salutis*. The goal of this paper is not to exhaust this glorious teaching, but only to summarize and recover some of its key distinctive elements as expressed in the past, in order to continue the conversation especially for Christian readers of today.

## The Foundational and Distinctive Tenets of the Doctrine

### The Doctrine of the Believer's Union with Christ

The English Puritan Edward Leigh defines the believers' union with Christ as a spiritual reality where, "Christ is said to dwell and abide in us, and we are said to be Christ's, to be partakers of Christ, to be clothed with Christ, and abide in him."[^2] The Genevan Reformer John Calvin, who predated Leigh, wrote similarly by heralding that "Christ is not external to us, but dwells in us; and not only unites us to himself by an undivided bond of fellowship, but by a wondrous communion brings us daily into closer connection, until he becomes altogether one with us."[^3] Put simply, the doctrine of union with Christ teaches that the Christian is in Christ, and that Christ, in His divine nature, is in him. This union should not be understood as physical or material, being a product of the interrelation of essences or accidents, but is spiritual and is rooted in the unsearchable mercy of God.[^4] As Girolamo Zanchi concurs, "Our union with Christ is spiritual in such a way that it is nevertheless true and real."[^5] In addition, every time the doctrine of the union of Christ is cited and employed, the context is always in relation to the redemption of sinners and the spiritual benefits they receive through that union. Thus, this doctrine is purely salvific and soteriological in its scope and use. In other words, the believers' union with Christ grounds and effects their salvation in and through Him. In this, Herman Bavinck affirms when he said, "all who are in communion with Christ are named 'Christian' and anointed to be prophets, priests and kings in him."[^6]

Central to the scriptural and exegetical grounds of the doctrine is the phrase *ἐν Χριστῷ*, which was prevalent in the New Testament, especially in the writings of the apostle Paul. As Campbell notes, "scholars unanimously regard this phrase to be central to the theme of union with Christ."[^7] In the Pauline corpus alone, there can be found seventy-three occurrences of this precise phrase *ἐν Χριστῷ*.[^8] Out of these occurrences, one notable theme that this phrase connotes is the idea of "things achieved for/given to people in Christ," which, upon closer inspection, speaks about the blessings of salvation towards the sinner. As Campbell summarizes:

> …the prepositional phrase *ἐν Χριστῷ*… is directly connected to Christ's work in relation to the following themes: redemption (Rom 3:24), the gift of eternal life (Rom 6:23; 2 Tim 1:1), sanctification (1 Cor 1:2), the gift of God's grace (1 Cor 1:4; 2 Tim 1:9), reconciliation (2 Cor 5:19), the gift of freedom (Gal 2:4), the gift of the blessing of Abraham to the Gentiles (Gal 3:14), the gift of every spiritual blessing (Eph 1:3), being raised with Christ and seated with him in the heavens (Eph 2:6), the creation of believers for good works (Eph 2:10), forgiveness (Eph 4:32), the provision of God (Phil 4:19), and salvation (2 Tim 2:10). God's acts toward his people are performed through Christ, or are conditioned by or associated with him. God's gifts toward his people are extended toward believers through Christ.[^9]

Aside from the phrase *ἐν Χριστῷ*, the doctrine of the believers' union with Christ can also be demonstrated through the phrases *ἐν αὐτῷ* (1 Cor 1:5; 2 Cor 1:19–20; Eph 1:4; Col 1:16; Col 2:10; 2 Thess 1:12), *σὺν Χριστῷ* (Rom 6:8; Col 2:20), *σὺν αὐτῷ* (2 Cor 13:4; Col 2:13; 1 Thess 5:10), *σὺν* compounds (Rom 6:4–5; Phil 3:10; Col 2:12; Eph 2:5; Phil 3:21; Col 3:1; Rom 8:17; Eph 2:6), and *διὰ Χριστοῦ* (Rom 1:5; 5:1, 11, 21; 7:4; 1 Cor 15:57; 2 Cor 5:18; Eph 1:5; 2:18; Rom 5:9; 1 Thess 5:9; Titus 3:6). In addition, the idea that Christ is united to the Christian is illustrated through some Pauline metaphors: being members in the body of Christ (Eph 1:22–23; 4:15–16; Col 1:18; 2:19; Eph 2:14; 5:23), the temple and the building (Eph 2:21–22), and marriage (Eph 5:25–29).[^10] Campbell is once again helpful when he sums up that:

> …every element of Christ's work…is connected in some way to union with Christ. Salvation, redemption, reconciliation, creation, election, predestination, adoption, sanctification, headship, provision, his death, resurrection, ascension, glorification, self-giving, the gifts of grace, peace, eternal life, the Spirit, spiritual riches and blessings, freedom, and the fulfillment of God's promises are all related to union with Christ… As Christ performs his works, he represents believers while in some spiritual sense they partake in the events that he undergoes. It is also personally encompassing in that a believer's whole person is defined by their participation in Christ's death, resurrection, ascension, and so forth. The uses of the metaphors…convey yet another type of union with respect to the work of Christ… God's people are portrayed as members of the body of which Christ is head; they are part of the building he constructs; they constitute the marriage partner for whom he cares and loves. The metaphors convey incorporation and unity, and they depict realities that have been created by Christ and continue to be nurtured by him.[^11]

With all of these salvific realities being affixed into this work of union, it's no wonder why pastors, theologians, and scholars put high prominence and worshipful glory to this doctrine of the believer's union with Christ. John Calvin was certainly among these men when he said that "so long as we are without Christ and separated from him, nothing which he suffered and did for salvation of the human race is of the least benefit to us. To communicate to us the blessings which he received from the Father, he must become ours and dwell in us."[^12] Some would even consider union with Christ the chief blessing the Christian receives from the triune God, through which all spiritual blessings flow. The particular Baptist John Gill, following the Puritan Thomas Goodwin, thought in this way when he writes:

> In a word, union to Christ is the first thing, the first blessing of grace flowing from love and effected by it; and hence is the application of all others; of him are ye in Christ Jesus, first loved and united to Christ, and then it follows, who of God is made unto us wisdom and righteousness, sanctification and redemption, 1 Cor. 1:30. So Dr. Goodwin observes, that "union with Christ is the first fundamental thing of justification and sanctification and all."[^13]

Furthermore, the Second London Baptist Confession of Faith emphasized the centrality of this theological principle when it begins the chapter of sanctification with the following words: "They who are united to Christ, effectually called, and regenerated, having a new heart and a new spirit created in them through the virtue of Christ's death and resurrection, are also farther sanctified…"[^14] While there is no chapter in the Confession dedicated to the doctrine of union with Christ, it is clear that, by this statement, the framers considered the teaching a fundamental axiom governing and upholding Reformed soteriology. To be united to Christ is the fountain of all other saving graces wherein sinners are made partakers. Without union, there is no redemption, and to have Christ is to have all.

### The Threefold Aspect of the Believers' Union with Christ

While the doctrine is widely received and accepted by Reformed theologians both past and present, it has been subject to some confusion and unwarranted nuances in the present, especially regarding where to locate this act of union in time and space. Some would posit that union with Christ should not be confined to the *ordo salutis* because of its comprehensiveness. Others are willing to say that the believer's union with Christ happened during His resurrection from the dead, and few would assert that the elect are united to Christ from eternity. While all of the aforementioned assertions may be true and can be verified through the witness of Scripture, a proper distinction must be established to avoid unnecessary and unreasonable conclusions.

Older theologians used to distinguish the single act of God in the salvation of sinners into three moments: eternity, history, and actuality. The Puritan Thomas Goodwin follows this line of thought in his doctrine of salvation, wherein he stated that "there are three sorts of works whereby our salvation is completed and accomplished."[^15] These three are:

1. Immanent in God towards us, as his eternal love set and passed upon us, out of which he chose us, and designed this and all blessings to us.
2. Transient, in Christ done for us; in all he did or suffered representing of us, and in our stead.
3. Applicatory, wrought in us and upon us, in the endowing us with all those blessings by the Spirit; as calling, justification, sanctification, glorification.[^16]

Consequently, this leads to the establishment of a threefold aspect of the believers' union with Christ. Following Goodwin and others, the first aspect would be the **immanent/decretal union** wherein, by God's decree of election, we are hereby elected upon union with Christ from all eternity, before the foundation of the world (Eph 1:3–4). The second aspect would be the **transient/federal union** wherein the believers' union with Christ was guaranteed through His incarnation, sufferings, and glories. In this union, those who are chosen are united to Christ by way of federal headship as He acted for us in His mediatorial death and resurrection (Rom 6:3–11). The third and last aspect would be the **applicatory/actual union**, or mystical union, which is the present union of the believers with Christ in time and space. In this "now union," the believers are united to Christ and are now recipients of all the spiritual and salvific blessings as wrought by the Spirit (Eph 2:5–6). John Gill certainly employs a similar way of theologizing when he said that "there is an union with Christ, which election in him gives; and there is a legal union between him and the elect, as between a surety and debtor, in virtue of suretyship-engagements for them; and there is a mystical union, as between head and members; and a conjugal one, as between man and wife."[^17]

Speaking broadly of the doctrine, there are certainly multiple angles through which believers are said to be united to Christ, all of which are necessary for the redemption of their souls. None can be united to Christ in time and space who has not been elected in Christ from eternity, and there is no actual union without Christ acting as their federal head through His Person and Work. But more importantly, "until he is born again, he has no knowledge of this blessing, no comfortable perception of it; nor can he claim his interest in it, nor have that peace and joy which flow from it."[^18]

## The Nature of The Believers' Mystical Union with Christ

It must be pointed out that within the threefold aspect of the believer's union with Christ, it is the third one, the applicatory/actual union, which Scriptures and theologians stress the most and expound in greater length. Also called the believer's mystical union with Christ, it is in this act wherein the believers actually experience all the graces of salvation. As Calvin wrote, "the mystical union, we assign the highest rank, Christ when he becomes ours making us partners with him in the gifts with which he was endued."[^19] But while the mystical union with Christ is broadly articulated and defended within the Reformed circles, it seems that there are visible theological loose ends when one attempts to locate this union in the logical order of the *ordo salutis*. This is especially true when one is pressed on the role of this mystical union with Christ in relation to regeneration and justification. Richard Muller, for example, in his *Dictionary of Latin and Greek*, defined *unio mystica* (mystical union) as both occurring with the believer (1) in and through regeneration, and (2) in and following justification.[^20] In recent years, discussions about the believer's mystical union with Christ in relation to faith have also become a hot topic.[^21] In these nuanced dialogues, the underlying question is: "Are we united to Christ in and through faith? Or are we united to Christ before faith?"

To try to elucidate this seemingly confusing quandary is to recover and reintroduce a helpful distinction in the aspect of the believer's mystical union with Christ. Some older theologians, especially the Puritans, were unyielding in their insistence that (1) there can be no union with Christ without the believer's faith and that (2) there can be no faith in the believer without being united to Christ first. With this line of theologizing, they would argue for a twofold aspect of the believer's mystical union with Christ.

First is the **objective union of Christ with the believer**. In this aspect of union, Christ unites Himself to the sinner objectively, apprehending the sinner unconsciously and then giving the Spirit to him. In the words of Goodwin, in this first aspect of the mystical union, Christ "takes hold of us before we believes…works a thousand and a thousand operations in our souls to which…Christ dwells in us and works in us, when we act not and know not our union, nor that it is he that works."[^22] Logically and narrowly speaking, this initial union occurs before or through regeneration (or effectual calling).

The second aspect of this mystical union can be rightly called the **subjective union of the believer with Christ**. When Christ unites Himself to the sinner, He then bestows the Spirit to him, thereby regenerating his heart, who in turn exercises faith towards Christ. With this conscious faith coming from God as a gift, the sinner is then united to Christ and is now ready to receive all other saving graces like justification, adoption, sanctification, perseverance, and glorification, thus completing the mystical union. Essentially, in this twofold mystical union, the resurrected and glorified Christ first takes the sinner into his custody, uniting Himself with him, and this, through the Spirit, will result in regeneration. The sinner then, as now being alive to God, will exercise true saving faith towards Christ, resulting in a complete union as he is now united to Himself, and through which the sinner now receives everything that Christ merited by the applicatory work of that same Spirit.

This line of thinking is not unique to Goodwin alone but extends to some of the eminent Puritans of the 17th century. Edward Leigh, for example, writes that "this union is wrought on God's part outwardly by the Word, and inwardly by the Spirit: on man's part, outwardly by our profession, inwardly by faith."[^23] Along this line, Thomas Manton similarly argues in a lucid manner:

> For since Union is said to be by Faith; Ephes. 3.17. That Christ may dwell in your Hearts by Faith. And Faith is an Act of Spiritual Life, it seemeth there is Life before our Union with Christ: So that this Union seemeth to be the Effect, rather than the Cause of the Spiritual Life, and some say it is the Effect of the Beginning, and the Cause of the Continuance and Increase of it… But I suppose this Method is not right: Briefly then, for the manner and order how it is wrought, take it thus: Union it is by the Spirit on Christ's part, and Faith on ours; he beginneth with us as the most worthy, as having a quickning and life-making Power in himself. 1 Cor. 15.45. The last Adam was made, *πνεῦμα ζῳοποιοῦν*, a quickning Spirit. By the Spirit he infuseth Spiritual Life, the first Act of which is Faith, that is the first Grace that acteth upon Christ, and maketh the Union reciprocal, that so in him we may have Righteousness and Grace. Phil. 3.9. And be found in him, not having mine own Righteousness which is of the Law, but that which is through the Faith of Christ, the Righteousness which is of God by Faith. All Graces flow from Union with Christ, so doth Faith. Believing is an Act of the Spiritual Life, but it is at the same instant of time, and not before. The first Band of Union is the Spirit, for the Gift of the Spirit is the Cause of Faith, and every Cause is before the Effect in Nature, tho not in Time…[^24]

With this careful distinction on the believer's mystical union with Christ, both propositions that say "we are united to Christ prior to faith"[^25] and "we are united to Christ through faith"[^26] hold true. In the words of Wilhelmus à Brakel, "There is no spiritual life apart from union with Christ, who is the life of the soul. Faith is the means whereby union with Christ comes about."[^27]

## Conclusion

The doctrine of the believer's union with Christ should not be treated like a hidden jewel only to be employed on special occasions. Since this is the first and principal grace leading to other saving graces, this truth must be preached regularly from the pulpit, taught intentionally to the pews, and must be the forefront doctrine whenever we counsel, exhort, rebuke, and encourage the flock entrusted to us. Yet, with this great doctrine comes the great responsibility of handling this truth with care and faithfulness. Proper distinctions must be made so that we do not collapse one glorious truth from another glorious truth. In this way, we can proclaim to our people that our salvation is not merely *from* Christ, but *in* Christ, and that having been apprehended by Him, we now cling to Him by faith, confident that nothing in all creation can separate us from the love of God as we are being sealed by and through our indissoluble union with Christ Jesus.
