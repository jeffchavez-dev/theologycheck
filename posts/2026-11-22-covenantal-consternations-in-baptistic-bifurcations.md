Within the realm of theology as it has developed in the Reformed tradition, covenant theology occupies a royal position. Reformed Christians consider it especially influential.[^1] Historically, it was "in seed form" in the early church and was especially developed "during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries," growing "out of a recognition that the Bible reveals God as dealing with His people by means of covenants."[^2] Thus, covenant theology is a covenantal, comprehensive framework both derived from and applied to the content of Scripture, recognizing and emphasizing what the Bible does.[^3] It is covenantal in that it deals with the covenants of Scripture; it is comprehensive in that the covenants "run from the beginning to the end of the Bible."[^4] This means that in order to rightly understand Scripture it is necessary to "have a good handle on what [it] says about the covenants."[^5]

If one's covenant theology is considered as a flag, that flag serves to signal his particular approach to and application of the totality of Scripture. It might also be thought of as a lens. And as a lens it affects and informs one's perspective on things like the pattern of redemptive history, God's purposes, and the interrelation of the Old and New Testaments, among many other things. But are all parties using the same glasses? Though the raw data is the same for everyone, the answer is a distinct no.

This is seen in the fact that covenant theology acts as one of the dividing lines between Baptists and Presbyterians. But why the difference?

Perhaps this question should be asked: from where does one's covenant theology come? From Scripture alone, from tradition, or from something else? Stated another way, how does one *arrive at* his given position on the covenants of Scripture? The answer is found in hermeneutics, the science and art of interpreting a document, in this case the Bible.[^6] However one interprets and understands the raw biblical data is ultimately going to drive his covenant theology.[^7] The aim of this paper is to do just that, to examine a part of a particular covenant theology in light of the Scripture. This covenant theology is known as 1689 Federalism. Before that critique can begin, however, some basic clarifications must follow.

## Definitions and Presuppositions

### A General Definition and Confessional Distinction

For the purposes of clarity and brevity, 1689 Federalism is best defined here at the outset as a Particular Baptist covenant theology. *Particular* refers to the doctrine of particular redemption, otherwise known as limited atonement.[^8] *Baptist* refers to the doctrine of credobaptism or the baptism of professing believers only. The word *federalism* is synonymous with *covenantalism*, as *federal theology* is synonymous with *covenant theology*.[^9] The qualifier *1689* is with reference to the 1689 London Baptist Confession of Faith. This Confession (LBCF) was formulated by Particular Baptists and based, in large part, upon the Savoy Declaration and the Westminster Confession of Faith (WCF).

Another general point of clarity is necessary to make: 1689 Federalism is *not* to be confused or conflated with 1689 Confessionalism. That is, all 1689 federalists hold to the 1689 Confession but *not* all 1689 confessionalists hold to 1689 Federalism. In other words, 1689 Federalism is not the exclusively required covenant theology of anyone who holds to the LBCF. This is important to note because the qualifier of 1689 suggests it is *the* covenant theology of the LBCF, but it is not. Thus, one may both subscribe to the LBCF and reject the covenant theology of 1689 Federalism in favor of another form of baptist covenant theology.

### Historical and Developmental Questions

Interestingly, there are some Calvinistic Baptist pastors who have objected to 1689 Federalism as being non-Reformed, noting that this charge, often made by Presbyterians toward Baptists, is not without reasonable warrant; and they have even questioned the historical methodology used to justify the system.[^10] This suggests the position is tentative, and perhaps not as historic as it is purported to be. In other words, the circumstances are questionable. These dissenting pastors hold to a second, and more typical, covenantal position among Particular Baptists which they dub "Reformed Baptist Covenantalism"[^11] or "Reformed Baptistic Covenant Theology."[^12] They believe this position, in ostensible contrast to 1689 Federalism, is "properly Reformed and properly historic."[^13] They also mention the presence of an interpretive bias among some 1689 Federalists that can be a "huge problem," going so far as to suggest this is "very dangerous," though they doubt this is done maliciously.[^14] This bias, they claim, makes its way into a few key 1689 Federalism works, namely the LBCF and the work of Nehemiah Coxe coupled with John Owen's.[^15] They say that these works have been "edited, revamped, and reinterpreted."[^16] Thus, it is alleged that some 1689 Federalists are failing to contextualize both Nehemiah Coxe and John Owen,[^17] both of whom they attempt to use as historical anchors. The point here is not to validate or invalidate these claims but merely to point to an apparent theological dilemma among Confessional Baptist brethren, and especially to show that some non-1689 Federalist Reformed Baptists consider 1689 Federalism to be somewhat of a novelty incompatible with the Reformed tradition; thus, something of a background and context is established.

### The Paper's Aspiration and Writer's Presuppositions

The purpose of this paper with respect to 1689 Federalism is not to engage with every minute point or expression endemic to the view. Such an endeavor would no doubt require a much larger work to adequately address the whole of the theology, its implications, and its myriad problems, although some of those issues may be alluded to or mentioned accordingly if deemed relevant to parts of the critique. Rather, this paper's main goal is to highlight some key aspects and emphases of 1689 Federalism and critically evaluate them in relation to Scripture and sound reason. Specifically, the system's view of the Mosaic Covenant will be addressed in the light of various scriptures which speak on the post-Exodus Israelites.

The basic presuppositions of the writer deemed pertinent to this study follow. Scripture is God's inspired, infallible, inerrant, authoritative, and sufficient Word. It is the sole rule of faith, life, and practice to which all traditions, teachings, confessions, creeds, councils, etc. are subject.[^18] Its inherent meaning is certain; its interpretation by man is not and is subject to error. Scripture interprets Scripture, Scripture cannot contradict Scripture, and the Holy Spirit is necessary to understand and receive Scripture aright. Basic to the study of theology is its fabric-like nature; pull one thread and the whole tapestry moves. This applies even to minor points, and has major implications. Thus, theology must be formed from and congruent with all of the biblical data. Any point of apparent contradiction is not with the Bible but with man, and must be resolved by careful and prayerful study. Thus, the student of Scripture must bow to the teaching of Scripture and conform his thinking, and therefore his theology, to its teaching, not the reverse. "And this will we do, if God permit."[^19]

### Final Preliminary Clarifications

Finally, this study, being that it is a critique, is necessarily polemical and perhaps on some points even dogmatic. No apologies are made for this. All men are dogmatic; some men just prefer to dogmatically insist they are not dogmatic. And last, many educational footnotes are included. This is so because there are related subjects and questions which cannot be directly addressed in the body of the paper but seemed important to mention, or at the very least to expound on briefly. It is also done, at times, for clarification purposes, and of course for citations and references. Thus, any reader is encouraged to engage with the footnote material in order to be stimulated toward fuller thinking and understanding.

## Examination and Consideration of 1689 Federalism vs. Post-Exodus Israelites

### The Mosaic Covenant (Mis)Conception

One of the fundamental distinctions and emphases of 1689 Federalism is in its conception of the very nature of the Mosaic Covenant. Being that this covenant was made at Sinai, rendered obsolete at the crucifixion of Christ, and received its final death blow in the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in AD 70, it necessarily serves as the backdrop and context for much of redemptive history and the Bible. Therefore, one's view of this covenant will inevitably create theological ripples in many places across the waters of Scripture. And it is here, with only one small part of this covenant, that the critique begins.

Dr. Samuel Renihan, on behalf of 1689 Federalism, asserts, "the Mosaic Covenant was a *covenant of works* for life in the land of Canaan" and "whether the Israelites enjoy the land sworn to their fathers depends on whether they keep the commandments."[^20] To be clear, 1689 Federalists do not believe it is *the* covenant of works, but *a* covenant of works. Which is to say, they believe in a form of Republicationism.[^21] This is corroborated by Pascal Denault when he says that "the covenant of works was *reaffirmed in the old covenant*…to *remind Israelites of the terms of the original covenant of works*."[^22] Oddly, it seems that Denault and other 1689 Federalists have the entirety of all Old Testament (OT) covenants in mind when they say "old covenant," not merely the Mosaic Covenant (MC) itself.[^23] Still, for the purposes here, the MC is the focus.

Because of their emphasis upon works in the MC, 1689 Federalists conceive of the MC offering life and blessings in the land of Canaan to the Israelites "upon the condition of works."[^24] This necessarily means that faith is *irrelevant* to the covenant. If that sounds like a strange and bizarre misrepresentation of their position, see what Dr. Renihan has to say in confirmation of this exact point: "The obedience demanded [under the Mosaic Covenant] was an *obedience an unbeliever could render*."[^25] Such a statement, if considered even for a short time, is both stunning and confusing.

Now, to be sure, there was a works principle in the MC.[^26] And, at first glance, the promises of blessings and threats of curses outlined in Deuteronomy[^27] may suggest to the reader that the MC is indeed a covenant of works. However, when considered in light of other texts of Scripture, and certain points of fundamental theology, which is basic to hermeneutics, this cannot be sustained. Indeed, if sustained it *must* contradict the Scripture.

### The Wilderness Generation

The first Scripture to be contradicted concerns the wilderness generation:

> "Take heed, brethren, lest there be in any of you an evil heart of *unbelief*, in departing from the living God. But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin. For we are made partakers of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence stedfast unto the end; while it is said, To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as in the provocation. For some, when they had heard, did provoke: howbeit not all that came out of Egypt by Moses. But with whom was he grieved forty years? was it not with them that had sinned, whose carcases fell in the wilderness? And to whom sware he that they should not enter into his rest, but to them that *believed not*? So we see that they could not enter in because of *unbelief*. Let us therefore fear, lest, a promise being left us of entering into his rest, any of you should seem to come short of it. For *unto us was the gospel preached, as well as unto them*: but the word preached did not profit them, *not being mixed with faith in them that heard it*. For we which have believed do enter into rest… Seeing therefore it remaineth that some must enter therein, and they to whom it was first preached *entered not in because of unbelief*… There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. Let us labour therefore to enter into that rest, lest any man fall after the same example of *unbelief*."[^28]

Here are the basic points to draw out from this passage:

1. The gospel was preached to the wilderness generation (4:2a).
2. It did not profit them, "not being mixed with faith" (4:2b).
3. They did not enter the rest of Canaan *because of unbelief*.

What does this have to do with 1689 Federalism? Everything. To begin, notice that this pericope says *six times*, as if God wants us to pay close attention, that the wilderness generation was faithless.[^29] And, it is repeatedly stated that they were forbidden from entering God's rest, which for them was the promised land of Canaan, *because of* this very unbelief.

Calvinistic Baptist John Gill, commenting on v. 19, agrees:

> "'So we see that they could not enter in'… To God's rest, *the land of Canaan*, for they died by the plague before the Lord, and their carcasses fell in the wilderness, before they came to it… and the reason was, 'because of unbelief;' their *distrust of God*, his power, and his providence; this instance is produced by the apostle, to show the evil nature of *unbelief*…which instance he further improves in the following chapter."[^30]

Now, it ought to stand without question that the land was tied to the MC as a peculiar blessing of the MC. This was ostensibly implied by Denault earlier when he associated Canaan with blessings. Surely, whatever blessings were to be given in the land, necessitates that the land was intended as a blessing? After all, it is called "the promised" land, and "rest" is a blessing, not a naked work.

Now *here is the point of contradiction*, which ought to present itself to any judicious mind: if the MC was a covenant of works, and if its blessings, including the land itself, were conditioned upon works, and if these works (obedience) were possible apart from faith even by an unbeliever, how is it that God forbade the wilderness generation from entering the land, not because of a failure to do works, but because of their unbelief? The very reason for forbiddance is at odds with the notion that the covenant was one of works.

Putting this into a statement of the premises and the conclusion may help to demonstrate the problem:

- **P1:** MC is a covenant of works.
- **P2:** MC blessings require works.
- **P3:** MC works are possible by an unbeliever.
- **P4:** MC blessings include the land and its rest.
- **P5:** Unbelief barred the wilderness generation from the land/rest.
- **C:** MC cannot be a covenant of works; P5 refutes P1–P4.

If the conclusion stands, and by all reasonable measures it must stand, 1689 Federalism, in its conception of the nature of the Mosaic Covenant, is directly opposed to, even contradicts, Scripture, specifically the passage given in Hebrews 3 and 4. This is but the beginning of the knots tied by this theology. The only way out of this knot is to engage in unwarranted and strange bifurcations. This, respectfully, is precisely what 1689 Federalists do to preserve their theology.[^31] This will be addressed. But before that can be done, some potential objections to the problem of the unbelief in the wilderness generation must be anticipated and answered.

### Greek Objections

One potential objection that might be raised is this: "The Greek words underlying the Authorized Version can be translated either *unbelief* or *disobedient*! So it's not certain whether it was because of unbelief or disobedience that they were forbidden from entering the rest in the land. Therefore, the proposed clarity is really an ambiguity!"

The objection can be readily answered. The Greek for "unbelief" in 3:12 and 3:19 is *ἀπιστίας* (apistias) and is always translated according to the connotation of *unbelief* in the KJV.[^32] Mounce notes that *apistia* and *pistia* (belief/faith) are "polar opposites" and that *apistia* is "the negated form of the noun *pistia*."[^33] Vine concurs.[^34] The word *ἀπειθέω* is translated as *believed not* in 3:18. Generally speaking, it can mean to *disbelieve* or *disobey* and is translated in the KJV as "believe not (8x), disobedient (4x), obey not (3x), unbelieving (1x)."[^35] The majority of the translations favor the connotation of *disbelieve*. Interestingly, 6 out of the 7 times it is translated according to the idea of *disobedience*, it is done so only in 1 Peter, where it appears in some measure to be synonymous with *disbelieve* or *believed not*.[^36]

Moving along, in 4:2 the word *πίστει* is translated as *faith* an overwhelming 239 out of 244 times in the NT.[^37] It is mentioned as something that was absent from or "not being mixed with" the wilderness generation's hearing[^38] with respect to the gospel being preached to them.[^39] In 4:6 and 4:11, the words *ἀπείθειαν* and *ἀπειθείας* are translated as *unbelief* and are defined as "obstinacy, obstinate opposition to the divine will."[^40] Elsewhere they take on the connotation of *disobedience*.[^41] Lastly, the 7th and final word, *πιστεύσαντες*, is translated in 4:3 as "have believed," referring to those among Paul's audience who had faith. There is a clear absence of ambiguity here, for the word overwhelmingly has the connotation of *believe* or *trust*; and of its 248 instances in the NT, it is translated as "believe (239x), commit unto (4x), commit to (one's) trust (1x), be committed unto (1x), be put in trust with (1x), be commit to one's trust (1x), believer (1x)."[^42]

Now, let it be granted that where either the connotation of *disbelieve* or *disobey* might apply, that *disobey* is taken to be the correct translation, and so the idea of works. One still must ask and answer: what of the instances where *only* the connotations of *disbelieve* are appropriate, namely of 3:12 and 3:19?

Verse 12 opens the pericope with a warning to the audience before moving to argue from the wilderness generation's wicked example. Verse 19 is speaking plainly of the wilderness generation and says, as if it should be self-evident to all, "So we see that they could not enter in because of unbelief." Now, add to this the *overwhelming evidence*, namely that of 4:2 and 4:3, wherein the indication is undoubtedly that this is referring to the connotation of *disbelieve* on the negative side and *believe* on the positive. What remains? 3:18, 4:6, and 4:11. The evidence is so obviously suggestive, and any fair handling of it must, at the very minimum, grant that *faith was a part of the reason* why the wilderness generation was forbidden from possessing the MC blessing of the land and rest of Canaan. Of course, this is what the Scripture plainly says.

To add a final bolster to this point, consider other scriptures. In 1 Corinthians 10:5 it is said that "with many of [the wilderness generation] God was not well *pleased*" and in Hebrews 11:6 it is said that "without faith it is impossible to *please* [God]." If God was "not well pleased" with them, it follows that they were without faith, unless we want to suppose, as the 1689 Federalist, that there is such a thing as a faithless obedience.[^43] There is no such thing, for faithless obedience is no obedience at all, but by nature is disobedience. Lastly, Jude 1:5 confirms the preceding witnesses: "the Lord…destroyed them [of the wilderness generation] that believed not." Surely the point is plain.

### The Attestation of Moses and Aaron

Holding all that has been established, it's now necessary to move to Moses and Aaron, and the time here will be familiar and corroborative. Moses and Aaron were, like the wilderness generation, forbidden from entering the land. Why? For works merely, apart from faith? If only Deuteronomy is consulted, that appears to be the case.[^44] But Numbers, which is where the event in question takes place and is recorded, bears things out favorably for the previous point, namely, "And the LORD spake unto Moses and Aaron, Because ye *believed me not*, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this congregation into the land which I have given them."[^45]

The Hebrew underlying the translation is אָמַן (*aman*) and is most often translated as *believe*.[^46] It is the same word used of Abraham, that "he *believed* in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness."[^47] Paul tells us that this was justification by faith.[^48] This is not to imply that Moses and Aaron lost their justification; that's not possible. But it is to say that at this moment, just as the text says, they did not believe God, specifically what he had only just commanded them in Numbers 20:7–8; and it is also to say that bifurcating faith here would open the door to bifurcating Abraham's, and thus upend all Christian religion.

The Septuagint (LXX) strengthens this point. It uses the word *ἐπιστεύσατε* here in Numbers 20:12, taken from the root word *πιστεύω* which overwhelmingly means *believe*.[^49] In the NT, *every* instance of *ἐπιστεύσατε* is translated *believe*.[^50] The specific word form in the LXX is only used in two other places within the OT: Deuteronomy 9:23 and Jeremiah 25:8. The former is translated by the KJV (from Hebrew, not the LXX) as *believed* and the latter as *heard*.

All this evidence is supremely suggestive of what has already been proven: unbelief barred Moses and Aaron from the MC blessing of the land, just as the wilderness generation. Judging whether these parties were saved or damned is not within the purview of this paper, though there is strong reason to believe Moses and Aaron were saved but many of the wilderness generation perished in sin.[^51] Secondarily, the importance of the relationship of faith to obedience is increasingly manifest, as is 1689 Federalism's inability to deal with this relationship in a way that satisfies the biblical data. But there is still a third party, immediately related, to consider in these things.

### The Attestation of Joshua and Caleb

The law demands a minimum of two or three witnesses.[^52] Here, then, is the third and positive one, found in Joshua and Caleb. The biblical data does not explicitly state that they had faith.[^53] But let reason have its due: does it need to? Consider what has already been said. Westminster's "good and necessary consequence" is here a helpful friend.[^54]

If the point about the wilderness generation's unbelief is true, then of logical necessity Joshua and Caleb must have had faith. Why is this necessarily the case? First, of preliminary consideration, the wilderness generation, including the spies who brought an evil report,[^55] are the presented antithesis to Joshua and Caleb in the events concerning these parties. Second, and more importantly, if it is true that the wilderness generation was forbidden from entering the land due to unbelief, then it follows that those who *did* enter, Joshua and Caleb, did so because they possessed and exercised faith.

Thus considered, the relationship of the two parties is readily evident and harmonious to anyone who is willing to have it, and the antithesis between the two comports with the point. Or, shall it be said that Joshua and Caleb *disbelieved* God, yet did some work, faithlessly so, and were rewarded the land for it, in contradiction not only with what is stated about the wilderness generation but also with how God works and how he rewards[^56] post-fall mankind? Or, shall it be said that Joshua and Caleb were neutral agents, devoid either of faith or unbelief in the LORD God and in his precious promises to them, yet they somehow managed to follow the LORD fully? Or, shall it be said that they did works perfectly, always, and never sinned, and thus were given the land?[^57] To even begin to entertain this thinking is not only convoluted, it eventuates in massive absurdities and upends fundamental doctrines.

### Imposition of Bifurcation

How, then, do 1689 Federalists resolve this massive tension? To both maintain their system and "Amen" these scriptures, they must engage in bifurcation; that is, they divide in two, or more, some key points endemic to the covenant. Dr. Renihan is unequivocal in this regard when he says that there is an "outward purity," an "outward forgiveness," an "earthly forgiveness" not "eternal forgiveness" in the MC.[^58] Yet, remarkably, he admits only one page later, in what is likely a generalization, that "Israel was utterly faithless"[^59] and seems not to recognize any contradiction between such an admission and 1689 Federalism's view of the MC. For what does Israel's faithlessness have to do with *anything* if the MC obedience *did not require faith*? Why would God forbid the wilderness generation from Canaan due to unbelief if faith (or its lack) was immaterial?

For the 1689 Federalist, the answer is simple: bifurcate faith into temporal and eschatological.[^60] Thus, on the one hand, it's supposed that the Abrahamic, Mosaic, and Davidic covenants "*did nothing to change the nature of the Israelites*…*did not grant them a new heart*…*provided no help for compliance with the demands of the covenant*" and yet on the other hand, "the sacrificial system [was] a way to forgive sins."[^61] How is this possible? How can the MC provide *no help*, yet simultaneously, in apparent contradiction, *contain help* in sacrificial provisions for sin, according to 1689 Federalism? Bifurcation.[^62]

If forgiveness can be merely earthly, why can't sin also be? But are these really the most pertinent distinctions? Are sin and forgiveness defined especially with regard to *time and space*, that is the temporal, or to *earth*, that is the geological, or are they moral and ethical in the main? Surely, yes, there is such a thing as an *outward sin*, as in the case of murder, and an *inward sin*, as in malice of the heart, but what makes sin distinctly sinful? The outward chiefly? The inward chiefly? Neither. It is the *moral element* that is most important. "Sin is any want of conformity unto, or transgression of, the law of God."[^63] Whether it be outward or inward is not the question here. The question is, can sin be disconnected from the moral? The answer is a clear *no*. So then what of forgiveness or faith or promises or the gospel?[^64] Are we also to bifurcate these?

### Multiplication of Contradiction

In returning to Dr. Renihan's assertion that the obedience rendered under the MC is an obedience an unbeliever could render, a flood of questions must come. This proposition can be restated this way: *in the Mosaic Covenant God was pleased with faithless works*. The madness of this proposition, the contradiction of Scripture that this posits, the damage that this does to the Christian faith is astounding. How, indeed, should God ever be pleased with such, with faithless works, being that without faith it is *impossible to please God*?[^65] And this, says 1689 Federalism, is the nature of his covenant? Shall naked faithless works please the thrice holy God? Shall the creature apart from a mediator and faith find any acceptance with Almighty YHWH? This is nonsense and vanity and dangerous. It must be asserted unequivocally that there is no such thing as a faithless work performed by a fallen man which God could ever possibly be pleased with, whether under Christ and his Apostles or under Moses and the Prophets. This is in great danger of undermining the faith, as Rev. Ruddell says, "We need to move away from thinking that by our works we can in any way please God" for such thinking "starts striking at the gospel."[^66]

Was God ever pleased with faithless obedience in the MC? No. The only obedience that the LORD God accepts from fallen man is an obedience which is the fruit of faith in him, and even then all of man's "best works are imperfect and defiled in the sight of God,"[^67] they cannot merit pardon of sin or eternal life, they cannot endure the severity of God's holy judgment.[^68]

So asserts Westminster:

> "Works done by unregenerate men, although, for the matter of them, they may be things which God commands, and of good use both to themselves and others; yet, because they proceed not from an heart purified by faith; nor are done in a right manner, according to the word, nor to a right end, the glory of God; they are therefore *sinful, and cannot please God*, or make a man meet to receive grace from God. And yet their neglect of them is more sinful, and displeasing unto God."[^69]

And so says Rev. Ruddell, that in Exodus 25–31 Moses goes up the mountain and receives:

> "all, every jot and tittle, with regard to the tabernacle, the sacrifices, all the ways whereby he's going to teach the people about Jesus Christ. They're going to learn grace in what Moses is now receiving from the LORD in 25–31. They're going to learn about a high priest, a mediator, that stands before them, betwixt them and God; and they're going to learn about sacrifices that stand in their stead; they're going to learn about sin offerings and what it means sacramentally to put your hands on the head of an animal so that, sacramentally, your sins are transferred to that animal and then the blood of that animal is spilled; they're going to learn about what it means to be thankful before the LORD; they're going to learn about their thank offerings whereby they are received into the presence of God to eat a meal, just like they were at Sinai when 70 of the elders went up into Sinai and sat before God and peaceably had a meal with him. They're going to learn all of those things, so *don't tell me that Sinai is works*, because they're going to learn about everything pertaining to that era's proclamation of Jesus Christ."[^70]

## Summarization of Examination

1689 Federalism's conception of the Mosaic Covenant as one of works is exegetically indefensible. This has been demonstrated by Scripture and the basic hermeneutical tool of the analogy of Scripture, by reason, and by the absurdity of the proposition, which, if closely examined, would do great damage to many doctrines. That unbelief in the wilderness generation[^71] is what barred them from the Mosaic Covenant blessing of the land of Canaan is plainly stated by the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture.[^72] This point is corroborated by the cases of Moses and Aaron, as well as Joshua and Caleb, who, serving as the antithesis to the unbelieving wilderness generation, did enter into the land; and the obvious inference is that they did so by faith.

The only way for 1689 Federalism to maintain its view of the Mosaic Covenant in light of these things is to engage in unwarranted bifurcations and/or a skewed emphasis of the elements of things like sin, forgiveness, and faith, and the outward, external, temporal, and earthly elements of such. This not only neglects or omits what ought to be primary, it also commits the common hermeneutical error of "ascribing too much of the carnal element to Judaism."[^73] Further, it eventuates in many absurdities, such that it is unblushingly alleged that faithless obedience was acceptable to God, and that necessarily so from Sinai to the Cross. This is both a contradiction in terms and a contradiction of their own Confession.[^74] But it is consistent with conceiving of the Mosaic Covenant as a covenant of works.

Granted, there is a works principle in the Mosaic Covenant, and it does have temporal ramifications. But this does not make it, by nature, an exclusively or even mainly temporal covenant, nor essentially a covenant of works. That is not the point of Deuteronomy's blessings and curses any more than children in the New Covenant are rewarded for obedience to parents with long life upon earth,[^75] or those who fail to examine themselves properly at the Lord's Table may be struck with various judgments, temporally.[^76] All such things are but what might be termed "covenant encouragements" to the people of God, for "God graciously encourages us…by blessing obedience and by chastising disobedience," this is "not some kind of quid pro quo," or "works-merit arrangement with God. That is still God's gracious condescension."[^77]

In plain words:

> "In short, the Mosaic covenant reminds us of the covenant of works, but it is not itself a covenant of works for sinners to fulfill. It is rooted in grace, in God's free choice to save Israel and then to provide guidance in what pleases Him."[^78]

Thus, all things considered, 1689 Federalism must be rejected as a system of theology incongruent with and in contradiction of God's Word, and even its proponents' own Confession. Perhaps here it is fitting to reconsider the questionable circumstances and development of this doctrine as mentioned by those Reformed Baptists who reject this theology, and whether there be any validity or significance to their claims? Is 1689 Federalism's view truly Confessional in light of LBCF 16.7? Is it "properly historic?" Is it "properly Reformed?" It appears difficult, due to these covenantal consternations, to give a conscionable affirmative to such questions. Let the reader understand.[^79]
