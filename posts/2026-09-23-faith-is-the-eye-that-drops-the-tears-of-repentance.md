John Murray's book *Redemption: Accomplished and Applied* has been my go-to resource and recommended book when discussing the glory of the Triune God's work in salvation. It is a wonderful work that shows the accomplishment of Christ's finish work for the redemption of the Father's elect, and the historical application of the Spirit in the lives of the same elect people of God. John Murray championed here the doctrine of the union of Christ and how it functions as the source of all the blessings of salvation. He writes,

> "Union with Christ is a very inclusive subject. It embraces the wide span of salvation from its ultimate source in the eternal election of God to its final fruition in the glorification of the elect. It is not simply a phase of the application of redemption; it underlies every aspect of redemption both in its accomplishment and in its application. Union with Christ binds all together and insures that to all for whom Christ has purchased redemption he effectively applies and communicates the same."[^1]

Furthermore,

> "Union with Christ is the central truth of the whole doctrine of salvation. All to which the people of God have been predestined in the eternal election of God, all that has been secured and procured for them in the once-for-all accomplishment of redemption, all of which they become the actual partakers in the application of redemption, and all that by God's grace they will become in the state of consummated bliss is embraced within the compass of union and communion with Christ."[^2]

There is no other central truth of salvation than Union with Christ. He placed union with Christ toward the end of his book because it is an all-embracing reality that holds up the entirety of salvation from eternity past to eternity future. He denies that it is a single step within the order of application. To this, I say, "Amen!"

When it comes to the order of salvation particularly between faith and repentance, he rightly notes that they are inseparable.

> "The interdependence of faith and repentance can be readily seen when we remember that faith is faith in Christ for salvation from sin. But if faith is directed to salvation from sin, there must be hatred of sin and the desire to be saved from it. Such hatred of sin involves repentance which essentially consists in turning from sin unto God. Again, if we remember that repentance is turning from sin unto God, the turning to God implies faith in the mercy of God as revealed in Christ. It is impossible to disentangle faith and repentance. Saving faith is permeated with repentance and repentance is permeated with faith. Regeneration becomes vocal in our minds in the exercises of faith and repentance."[^3]

Indeed, saving faith is a penitent faith, and repentance is a believing repentance.

But when it comes to the question whichever is prior, he rejects any notion of priority,

> "The question has been discussed: which is prior, faith or repentance? It is an unnecessary question and the insistence that one is prior to the other futile. There is no priority. The faith that is unto salvation is a penitent faith and the repentance that is unto life is a believing repentance."[^4]

Is this understanding of conversion (faith and repentance) consistent with the entire biblical data and the reformed tradition?

## Faith is the eye that drops the tears of repentance

We must note that faith and repentance are simultaneous in time, but faith is prior in the "order of nature." Faith serves as the root and cause of true evangelical repentance. We must take into account the following biblical passages that demonstrate this.

Jeremiah 31:19 — *For after I turned back, I repented; And after I was instructed, I smote on my thigh; I was ashamed and also humiliated Because I bore the reproach of my youth.*

Here we see that words "After I was turned/converted, I repented") confirms that turning to God and repenting from sin is a sovereign work of God. Jeremiah was turned by God. And this can be understood that turning to God in faith logically precedes evangelical repentance. Without first being turned by God through faith, true godly sorrow and evangelical turning from sin are impossible. Edward Fisher writes,

> "3dly, No man can turn to God, unless he is first turned by God. And after he is turned, he repents. So Ephraim says, 'After I was converted, I repented,' Jer. 31.19. The truth is, a repentant sinner first believes that God will do what he promises, namely, pardon his sin and take away his iniquity, and then he rests in the hope of it. From that, and for it, he leaves sin, and forsakes his old course of life..."[^5]

John Gill rightly commented on this passage that "this is what is called evangelical repentance, and is from the grace of God; springs from love; flows from a sight of God and a view of Christ."[^6] Faith, which is a sight of Christ is the source from which true repentance flows.

The second text is from Zechariah 12:10 "they shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn" demonstrate that believing in God's pardoning mercy in Christ is what melts the heart into genuine godly sorrow. This Messianic passage is quoted in the New Testament by the apostle John (John 19:37) that it was the Messiah that they pierced and crucified. People will see Christ, the long-awaited Messiah pierced before the eyes of the transgressors. But the passage from Zechariah shows more that physical sight of the Messiah's death fulfilled in the New Testament. It is followed by the piercing words "they shall mourn." This language is a language of repentance. And looking upon the pierced One is faith.

Again, we can see this in John Gill's commentary on Zechariah 12:10, "Evangelical repentance springs from faith, and is accompanied with it… sin is a bitter thing, and makes work for bitter repentance."[^7]

When we see Christ by faith as the One who was pierced because of our sins, godly sorrow follows. Faith cries penitently.

> "...and looking upon Christ, 'whom he has pierced, he mourns bitterly for him, as one mourns for his only son,' Zech. 12.10. Thus, when faith has bathed a man's heart in the blood of Christ, it is so mollified that it quickly dissolves into tears of godly sorrow..."[^8]

Thomas Boston notes that "if one inquires about the way he gives repentance to Israel, the prophet Zechariah showed it before to be by faith."[^9] It is right to say that while "the law commands the tears of penitential sorrow to flow; the gospel, and the astonishing grace promised and offered in it, cause them to flow."[^10]

The last text is from the New Testament, Acts 11:21 "…who believed turned to the Lord." This explicitly shows that believing is before turning to the Lord. Believing in Christ is the root that enables a soul to turn from sin, turn to God, and cling to the Lord Jesus Christ with renewed purpose of the heart.

There are other passages that shows the priority of faith to repentance: Ezekiel 36:31 ("Then shall ye remember your own evil ways... and shall loathe yourselves") shows that hatred one's sin and evangelical mourning occur after seeing God's pardoning love in Christ by faith, Luke 7:37–47 (The Sinful Woman at Jesus's Feet) demonstrates that the woman's tears of repentance and love flowed as an effect of believing her sins were forgiven, and Luke 22:61–62 (Peter's Bitter Weeping), this illustrates that right evangelical repenting occurs when faith beholds the merciful look of Christ.

Those texts shows that faith is the eye that drops the tears of repentance. We do not separate faith and repentance but faith is logically prior to repentance for we cannot see the hidden darkness and loathiness of sin apart from believing what God says about Christ who died for our sins.

Though we have cited reformed theologians above in their commentary on various texts to prove our point, let me further provide citations from other key theologians.

John Calvin affirms that repentance is born of faith.

> "Now it ought to be a fact beyond controversy that repentance not only constantly follows faith, but is also born of faith... Yet, when we refer the origin of repentance to faith we do not imagine some space of time during which it brings it to the birth; but we mean to show that a man cannot apply himself seriously to repentance without knowing himself to belong to God."[^11]

The puritan Thomas Watson, while acknowledging that act of repentance is first seen in the believers, nevertheless affirms that it won't be displayed in the first place apart from faith in the heart.

> "I shall not dispute the priority, whether faith or repentance goes first. Doubtless repentance shows itself first in a Christian's life. Yet I am apt to think that the seeds of faith are first worked in the heart. When a burning taper is brought into a room, the light shows itself first, but the taper preceded the light. In the same way, we see the fruits of repentance first, but the beginnings of faith were there before."[^12]

Here are some quotes from Systematic Theologians affirming this assertion:

> "Unless faith be limited to some of its special exercises there can be no question that in the order of nature it must precede repentance. Repentance is the turning of the soul from sin unto God, and unless this be produced by the believing apprehension of the truth it is not even a rational act."[^13]

> "Calvin insists, one can never bring about the fruit of repentance until one first trusts in God as a merciful Father, and this can be derived only from the confidence that we are fully accepted in Christ."[^14]

> "Though faith and repentance appear simultaneously in all the saved, is there a causal priority or logical order between them? Calvin and several Reformed orthodox divines said that, though guilty fears and some moral reformation may precede faith, saving repentance logically follows faith and depends on it as its cause... An important concern of these divines was that we must not bind sinners to perform good works or feel deep penitence before they may come to Christ."[^15]

> "Consequently, first in the spiritual life comes faith... In this particular persuasion is the apex and choicest fruit of saving faith. And in particular: (1) it is the sharpest spur for all promptness and alacrity in piety, inciting a person to repentance (2 Cor. 7:1)"[^16]

> "If we speak in terms of causality, no doubt faith would come first, but temporally both arise at the same time by the acting of divine grace."[^17]

Thomas Boston in some of his notes in Edward Fisher's *The Marrow of Modern Divinity* cites the following theologians to argue that the grace that works in our hearts by the Spirit wrought faith:

> "Faith or believing, in order for the work of grace, is before repentance, that being the first and mother grace of all others; yet it is here and in other places named the latter: first, because though faith is first wrought, yet repentance is first seen and evidenced..."[^18]

> "What is your repentance? The effect of this faith, working a sorrow for my sins that are by-past, and purpose to amend [my ways] in time to come."[^19]

Finally, let's hear from some of the contemporary theologians, Ferguson, Sproul and Riddlebarger affirming the priority of faith in the order of nature to repentance, that repentance is an effect and immediate fruit of true faith,

> "At the end of the day we cannot divide faith and repentance chronologically. The true Christian believes penitently, and he repents believingly... But in the order of nature, in terms of the inner logic of the gospel and the way its 'grammar' functions, repentance can never be said to precede faith."[^20]

> "When someone is brought to faith by the Holy Spirit, he undergoes a conversion. His life turns around. This turning around is called 'repentance,' and it is an immediate fruit of genuine faith."[^21]

> "The Reformed, then, have historically linked faith, repentance, and obedience together, not calling the latter two elements within saving faith itself, but understanding them as corollaries within the ordo salutis. That is, one who has exercised faith in Christ, and is united to Christ by that faith, will repent and will struggle to obey and yield. But these things are not conditions for nor component parts of faith itself. They are fruits of saving faith. They are the inevitable activity of the new nature. They are 'effects'—signs that there has been an exercise of saving faith. They are not constituent parts of faith itself."[^22]

It should also be noted that the wording of the Second London Baptist confession of faith shows this logical order.

> 1689 LBCF 15.3 — This saving repentance is an evangelical grace, whereby a person, being by the Holy Spirit made sensible of the manifold evils of his sin, doth, BY FAITH IN CHRIST, humble himself for it with godly sorrow, detestation of it, and self-abhorrency, praying for pardon and strength of grace, with a purpose and endeavour, by supplies of the Spirit, to walk before God unto all well-pleasing in all things.

Chapter 15 is about repentance which comes after the chapter on Saving Faith (ch. 14). It maintains that the one made sensible of the evils of sin humbles himself by faith in Christ. This is accompanied with godly sorrow, hatred of sin and self. Also notice the Baptist Catechism's order

**Baptist Catechism (or Keach's Catechism 1695)**

Q. 89 What doth every sin deserve?
A. Every sin deserveth God's wrath and curse, both in this life and that which is to come. Faith and Repentance

Q. 90. What doth God require of us, that we may escape his wrath and curse due to us for sin?
A. To escape the wrath and curse of God due to us for sin, God requireth of us faith in Jesus Christ, repentance unto life, with the diligent use of all the outward means whereby Christ communicateth to us the benefits of redemption.

Q. 91. What is faith in Jesus Christ?
A. Faith in Jesus Christ is a saving grace, whereby we receive and rest upon him alone for salvation, as he is offered to us in the gospel.

Q. 92. What is repentance unto life?
A. Repentance unto life is a saving grace, whereby a sinner, out of a true sense of his sin, and apprehension of the mercy of God in Christ, doth, with grief and hatred of his sin, turn from it unto God, with full purpose of, and endeavour after, new obedience.

That's why Benjamin Keach can say,

> "And the Grace of God being thus poured forth upon the Soul by the Spirit, Faith is wrought in him to look to Jesus Christ; and so seeing and admiring the rich Bounty, Pardon, and Unwordable Clemency of his offended and provoked Sovereign, this works Repentance, or Godly Sorrow for Sin in every such Person..."[^23]

As we conclude this, within the *ordo salutis*, faith is not prior to repentance in time, nor does repentance precede faith. We can say that they are simultaneous, inseparable graces of conversion. But logically, faith is the eye that beholds Christ, the redeemer of God's elect, enabling the soul to exercise true, evangelical repentance. We can also draw various practical implications by maintaining the logical priority of faith to repentance.

First, this preserves the free, unconditional Offer of the Gospel. If repentance or forsaking sin were required before believing in Christ, preachers could not make a full and free offer of Christ to all hearers. This means mean that the offer would be restricted to those who had reformed themselves through repentance. Ferguson notes,

> "The fallacy here? The subtle movement from seeing forsaking sin as the fruit of grace that is rooted in election, to making the forsaking of sin the necessary precursor for experiencing that grace. Repentance, which is the fruit of grace, thus becomes a qualification for grace. This puts the cart before the horse. It stands the gospel on its head so that the proclamation of the gospel, with the call to faith in Christ, becomes conditional on something in the hearer."[^24]

Second, humbled sinners are relieved from the trap of self-examination before coming to Christ. This is indeed pastoral. Sinners who realize they cannot produce genuine godly sorrow, hate and loathe theirs sins on their own are saved from spiritual despair when they are called to go directly to Christ to receive the grace of repentance from Him. Examining oneself requires faith in what God says about sinners who alone can be saved by Christ. Boston, again, is helpful,

> "...to tell sinners that none may come to Christ, or have warrant to believe, except those who have true repentance — he must in a special way entangle distressed consciences, so that they dare not believe until they know that their repentance is true repentance."[^25]

Lastly, this understanding empowers a reformed and sanctified life. A sinner cannot truly forsake sin or yield acceptable and pleasing obedience until united to Christ by faith. Faith draws spiritual strength from Christ alone by the Spirit to cleanse the heart and mortify sin. The power to cry penitently is drawn from faith in Christ.

We can go on and enumarate the pastoral and practical benefits of seeing the logical priority of faith to repentance. Again, they are "twin sisters", but even when twins are born, somebody has to come first.

In the Philippines, the Tagalog word for repentance is "pagsisisi." A helpful mnemonic device to help remember the nature of repentance is using this word: "pag-C-C-C" (sounds similar to "pagsisisi). This stands for Confession, Conviction, and Change. I would say that no one can confession his sin apart from faith, no one can be convicted of sin without faith, and no one can be changed without first looking upon Christ's blood which alone can cleanse us from our sins.

> "The truth is, a repentant sinner first believes that God will do that which he promiseth, namely, pardon his sin, and take away his iniquity; then he rests in the hope of it; and from that, and for it, he leaves sin, and will forsake his old course, because it is displeasing to God..."[^26]

Some may not agree with this conclusion, and others may argue that faith depends on repentance that "Without repentance, there can be no faith. . . . For no man can choose his sins and Christ too."[^27]

But as Tom Hicks says elsewhere that "The Marrow theology is nothing but Reformed confessional theology. It is not a flavor of Reformed confessionalism. It is Reformed confessionalism."

> "Repentance is *by faith.* That is you must have Christ to have any of His graces. You must have Him before you can repent. Thus you must not forsake sin before coming to Christ but you must go to Christ by faith to forsake sin… 'Turning' from sin without having Christ would be antinomian. A false turning!"[^28]

Nevertheless, as mentioned above, we should not delay calling a sinner to flee to Christ unless we see the fruit of genuine repentance in them. Every preacher must call sinners to repent and believe. To bid sinners, "Come to the Savior, make no delay; Here in His word He's shown us the way; Here in our midst He's standing today, Tenderly saying, 'Come!'"
