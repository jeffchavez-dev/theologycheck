Every Reformation Month, our churches are tempted to celebrate the same five or six slogans and call it a day. *Sola Scriptura*, *sola fide*, a nod to Luther's hammer, and we move on. So it was a welcome surprise to see Word Community Church, at Ayala Malls Circuit in Makati, spend October 24–25, 2025 pointing us somewhere older than Wittenberg. "For Us and For Our Salvation: Confessing the Trinity for Life and Ministry" did not ask us to relitigate justification. It asked us to reckon with Nicaea, and with the God the Nicene Creed confesses.

That choice of focus is itself worth pausing on. The Reformers never saw themselves as founders of a new church; they saw themselves as recovering the faith once delivered to the saints, a faith already summarized for them in the ecumenical creeds. A conference that uses Reformation Month to send us back to 325 and 381 is not wandering off-theme. It is doing exactly what the Reformers did: reaching for the church's oldest, most tested confession of who God is, because the gospel cannot survive a defective doctrine of God. That is the conviction running under all six sessions, and it is why the conference is relevant far beyond the people who sat in the room.

**Session 1** set the terms. Ptr. James Chu opened with Sinclair van Dixhorn's observation that cults hide what they believe, while only Trinitarian Christians can say the Nicene Creed and mean every word of it. Chu gave three reasons the creed still matters: it is scripturally grounded, not scripture-adjacent; it is part of our family inheritance, functioning like a grammar book that teaches us how the faithful before us spoke of God; and familiarity with it is itself an exercise in honoring the fifth commandment, honoring the fathers who handed the faith down to us. His suggested uses were refreshingly unglamorous: confess it in worship, get familiar with it, memorize it, and don't fret over doing so. Jaroslav Pelikan's question hovered over the whole session: what do you value most in the Nicene Creed? For Chu, it was the first word — "We." The creed was never meant to be recited alone.

**Session 2**, on the Father Almighty, pressed into classical theism proper. Ptr. Ferrer's line deserves to be framed: "God's singularity and simplicity allows us to understand the Trinity better." That is not a hedge against the Trinity; it is the precondition for confessing it rightly. Scott Swain's reminder that "Christian praise of the Trinity is praise of the one God" and Kevin DeYoung's *The Good News We Almost Forgot* both did the same work — recovering divine simplicity and aseity (Psalm 90:1–4) not as scholastic trivia but as an invitation to behold God as He is. The application was pastoral, not academic: believing in the one true God compels us to behold Him, to know Him deeply as preparation for the beatific vision, to entrust this knowledge to the next generation, and to build biblically healthy churches on it.

**Session 3** was the theological center of gravity. Ptr. Jerome Ocampo took up "Very God of Very God" with Hilary of Poitiers' prayer as his starting point and Arius's error as his foil. Eternal generation, he argued, is the Father's *ad intra* act of originating the person of the Son while communicating the same undivided substance — origination without time, without subordination, without creation. Gregory of Nazianzus's blunt line, "there was never a time when the Father was without a Son," and Gregory of Nyssa's insistence that there is no time interval in begetting, both guard the same mystery that Bavinck called "the lifeblood of dogmatics." Ocampo was careful to distinguish analogy from identity — Augustine's warning that "every analogy means some kind of likeness," never equivalence — and to walk through the doctrinal stakes: deny eternal generation and you eventually deny the Son's aseity, His immutability, and God's simplicity itself. As he put it, "the doctrine of eternal generation establishes the uniqueness of Jesus as the only one qualified to provide redemption." The Q&A that followed, probing Calvin's language of personal versus essential communication, autotheos, and James White's contested stance on simplicity, showed a room unwilling to let these questions stay theoretical.

**Session 4** turned from the Son's eternal generation to His mission. Ptr. John Hofileña's line that "the Nicene Creed remains vital in a time where Jesus is most familiar yet not rightly understood" names our exact cultural moment — a Jesus everyone quotes and almost no one confesses rightly. Against Arianism, Apollinarianism, adoptionism, and Docetism, and against their modern cousins in liberal Christology's "quest for the historical Jesus" and New Age spirituality's Jesus-as-form, Hofileña anchored everything in Gregory of Nazianzus's axiom, "that which he has not assumed he has not healed." DeYoung again supplied the sharpest line: "The Son didn't come to make people better but to make dead people live." The incarnation, on this account, is less a geography lesson than a display of grace — the Son who did not stop being rich by becoming poor.

**Session 5**, on the Holy Spirit, gave Ptr. Raffy Garcia room to correct a real deficiency in Reformed pneumatology at street level. Citing Barna's finding that many self-identified Christians treat the Spirit as an impersonal force, Garcia warned that losing sight of the Spirit's personhood turns Him into "a genie that can be manipulated, not a God who has to be honored." His treatment of the *filioque* controversy, and Calvin's language of "distinction without division," modeled how to hold East and West in charitable tension rather than easy dismissal. The practical payoff was Christ-centered: we worship the Spirit rightly by directing our attention to the Son.

**Session 6** closed the conference where ecclesiology belongs — under the doctrine of God. Ptr. Derick Parfan built on John Webster's principle that "there can be no doctrine of the church that is not fully about God," then walked through the four marks — one, holy, catholic, apostolic — as a corrective to our era's individualism. Cyprian's dictum, echoed by Augustine and Calvin, that "you cannot have God as your Father if you do not have the church as your mother," is a hard word for low-ecclesiology evangelicalism to hear, but it is precisely the word this conference was built to deliver.

Taken together, these six sessions make a simple but urgent case: a church that cannot confess the Nicene Creed will eventually lose the gospel it was written to protect. That is the conference's real relevance — not nostalgia for old words, but recovery of the only grammar precise enough to keep us worshiping the God who is actually there.
