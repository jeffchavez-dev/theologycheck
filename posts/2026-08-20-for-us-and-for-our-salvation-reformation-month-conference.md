**Session 1** set the terms. Ptr. James Bryner Chu opened with van Dixhorn's observation that cults hide what they believe, while only Trinitarian Christians can confess the Nicene Creed and mean every word of it. Ptr. James gave three reasons why the creed still matters: (1) it is scripturally grounded, not scripture-adjacent;  (2) it is part of our family inheritance, functioning like a grammar book that teaches us how the early Christians spoke of God; (3) and familiarity with it is itself a mode of honoring the fifth commandment, honoring the fathers who handed the faith down to us. 

He ended with a suggestion on the practical use of the creed: public confession in worship, personal familiarity, contemplative memorization, and persistence in doing so. In our home, this has become a part of our family worship. Gladly, there are helpful ways to memorize the creed. One way is to [sing it](https://youtu.be/N7ZQfNjvDuw?si=4fvD6KRjJmo-eXOZ)! Jaroslav Pelikan's question nailed the whole session: what do you value most in the Nicene Creed? For Ptr. James, it was the first word, "We." 

**Session 2**, on the Father Almighty, Ptr. Ferrer pressed into classical theism proper with this line:* "God's singularity and simplicity allow us to understand the Trinity better."* This captures the long-held tradition's commitment to the fact that Simplicity as the divine identity is a Trinitarian grammar. We believe in *one* God. In other words, Simplicity is a precondition for confessing the Trinity rightly. Scott Swain's reminder that "Christian praise of the Trinity is praise of the one God" and Kevin DeYoung's *The Good News We Almost Forgot* both did the same work. The work of recovering divine simplicity and aseity (Psalm 90:1–4) is not a scholastic riddle but an invitation to behold God as He is in Himself. Ptr. Ferrer's application was pastoral, not academic. He advances the belief that the one true God is first the *Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth*. This compels us to behold Him as the one who has deep affection for His people. To know Him deeply prepares us for the beatific vision. We do well to pass this knowledge to the next generation, and to build biblically healthy churches on it.

**Session 3** was, for me, the theological center of the conference. Ptr. Jerome Ocampo took up "Very God of Very God," using Hilary of Poitiers' prayer as his starting point and contrasting it with Arius's error. Eternal generation, he argued, is the Father's *ad intra* act of originating the person of the Son while communicating the same undivided substance. This is an origination without time, without subordination, without creation. Gregory of The line, "there was never a time when the Father was without a Son" robustly supports this doctrine. If there was a time when the Son was not, it would follow then that there was a time when the Father had no Son, which is a clear antithesis of what the Scripture principally teaches. Gregory of Nyssa's insistence that there is no time interval in begetting, both guard the same mystery that Bavinck called "the lifeblood of dogmatics." Ocampo was careful to distinguish analogy from identity — Augustine's warning that "every analogy means some kind of likeness," never equivalence — and to walk through the doctrinal stakes: deny eternal generation and you eventually deny the Son's aseity, His immutability, and God's simplicity itself. As he put it, "the doctrine of eternal generation establishes the uniqueness of Jesus as the only one qualified to provide redemption." The Q&A that followed, probing Calvin's language of personal versus essential communication, autotheos, and James White's contested stance on simplicity, showed a room unwilling to let these questions stay theoretical.

**Session 4** turned from the Son's eternal generation to His mission. Ptr. John Hofileña's line that "the Nicene Creed remains vital in a time where Jesus is most familiar yet not rightly understood" names our exact cultural moment — a Jesus everyone quotes and almost no one confesses rightly. Against Arianism, Apollinarianism, adoptionism, and Docetism, and against their modern cousins in liberal Christology's "quest for the historical Jesus" and New Age spirituality's Jesus-as-form, Hofileña anchored everything in Gregory of Nazianzus's axiom, "that which he has not assumed he has not healed." DeYoung again supplied the sharpest line: "The Son didn't come to make people better but to make dead people live." The incarnation, on this account, is less a geography lesson than a display of grace — the Son who did not stop being rich by becoming poor.

**Session 5**, on the Holy Spirit, gave Ptr. Raffy Garcia room to correct a real deficiency in Reformed pneumatology at street level. Citing Barna's finding that many self-identified Christians treat the Spirit as an impersonal force, Garcia warned that losing sight of the Spirit's personhood turns Him into "a genie that can be manipulated, not a God who has to be honored." His treatment of the *filioque* controversy, and Calvin's language of "distinction without division," modeled how to hold East and West in charitable tension rather than easy dismissal. The practical payoff was Christ-centered: we worship the Spirit rightly by directing our attention to the Son.

**Session 6** closed the conference where ecclesiology belongs — under the doctrine of God. Ptr. Derick Parfan built on John Webster's principle that "there can be no doctrine of the church that is not fully about God," then walked through the four marks — one, holy, catholic, apostolic — as a corrective to our era's individualism. Cyprian's dictum, echoed by Augustine and Calvin, that "you cannot have God as your Father if you do not have the church as your mother," is a hard word for low-ecclesiology evangelicalism to hear, but it is precisely the word this conference was built to deliver.

Taken together, these six sessions make a simple but urgent case: a church that cannot confess the Nicene Creed will eventually lose the gospel it was written to protect. That is the conference's real relevance — not nostalgia for old words, but recovery of the only grammar precise enough to keep us worshiping the God who is actually there.
